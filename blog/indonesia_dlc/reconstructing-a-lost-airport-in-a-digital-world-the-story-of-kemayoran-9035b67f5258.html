Reconstructing a Lost Airport in a Digital World: The Story of Kemayoran

There are places that disappear without truly vanishing.

Kemayoran Airport is one of them.

Today, most of what used to be Jakarta’s primary international gateway has been absorbed into the urban sprawl. The runways are long gone, the terminal buildings have mostly been demolished or repurposed, and an enormous aviation complex is now sliced through by busy city roads.

Yet, if you know where to look, Kemayoran is still there.

My initial awareness of Kemayoran came from a surprisingly thin set of facts: I knew it was abandoned; I knew its grounds roughly aligned with Benyamin Sueb Road; I knew its distinctive air traffic control towers held legendary status among local aviation geeks; and, like many, I remembered seeing it featured in Tintin and the Flight 714.

That was about it.

But the deeper I dug, the more compelling the site became.

An Airport Buried Beneath Modern Jakarta

What makes Kemayoran so striking today is the stark contrast between its historical footprint and its current environment.

Benyamin Sueb Road, which cuts directly through the old airfield, is a massive arterial corridor — spanning roughly eight lanes wide, and even wider depending on how you count the surrounding carriageways and medians. Driving down it today, it takes a real stretch of the imagination to picture commercial airliners touching down on that exact patch of land.

That layer of history hidden right under the surface is what makes the location so fascinating. Kemayoran isn’t just a dead piece of infrastructure; it has become an archaeological stratum of modern Jakarta.

The most vivid reminders of this past are the two surviving ATC towers. Left behind as the city grew around them, they now stand encircled and slowly engulfed by overgrown vegetation. There is a quiet poetry to it: structures built to manage the skies above one of Southeast Asia’s busiest cities are now being quietly reclaimed by the foliage below.

What If Kemayoran Had Never Disappeared?

My curiosity shifted into a serious project when I began looking at the site through the lens of GIS, Earth Observation, and spatial Digital Twins.

A true Digital Twin isn’t just a static 3D model. It’s a dynamic spatial system — capturing the relationships between terrain, buildings, transit corridors, and infrastructure to understand how a environment actually operated. Kemayoran presented the perfect sandbox for this concept.

Instead of just building an aircraft model, why not reconstruct the entire airport system?

I started with the main terminal. Drawing from archive photos and historical records, I drafted initial 3D block-outs. These weren’t meant to be final, definitive models; they were working hypotheses. How were the passenger flows organized? Where did the aprons meet the gates? How did ground support equipment move around the building?

Working through those geometric questions naturally forced me to dive deeper into the operational history of the airfield.

Mapping the Geometry of Aviation History

Reconstructing the physical structures was only half the equation; I needed to understand the rules the airport operated by.

I began analyzing historical aeronautical documentation, including old Jeppesen charts and archival material from the Directorate General of Civil Aviation. Charts offer something photographs can’t: a clear blueprint of operational logic.

Runways aren’t just strips of concrete. They are defined by magnetic alignments, approach vectors, taxiway links, navigation aids, and complex air traffic procedures within a larger airspace envelope. Once you learn to read an airport through its charts, you stop seeing a collection of isolated buildings and start seeing a functional machine.

This realized perspective sparked a broader counterfactual question: What if Kemayoran had survived?

Rather than imagining a scenario where it remained Jakarta’s sole international hub — which would be historically unrealistic — I began exploring a multi-airport system where Kemayoran coexisted alongside Halim Perdanakusuma and Soekarno-Hatta (Cengkareng).

Historically, Halim and Kemayoran already overlapped during the transition era of the 1970s and 80s. What if Kemayoran had been repurposed rather than shuttered? Could it have evolved into a high-density downtown hub, a regional commuter airport, or a dedicated business aviation center?

Reframing the project around these questions shifted the goal post. The core challenge was no longer just “What did it look like?” but rather “How could it have functioned in a modern aviation network?”

The Digital Toolkit: Blender & X-Plane

To bring this hypothesis to life, I am combining two distinct software workflows:

1. Architectural Geometry in Blender

Blender handles the detailed structural and spatial reconstruction. It allows me to iterate fluidly, building out the terminal and surrounding landscape at varying levels of detail as new reference photos or surveyed footprints come to light.

The beauty of digital reconstruction is that iteration is built into the process — being wrong on the first draft just means refining the mesh on the second.

2. Operational Simulation in X-Plane (WED)

While Blender handles visual fidelity, X-Plane’s World Editor (WED) builds the functional environment. WED introduces real-world spatial logic: runways need correct surface friction and lighting, taxiways require active routing nodes, and parking stands must align with aircraft wingspans.

Using both tools ensures the project is not just visually convincing, but operationally viable within a flight simulator environment.

Bringing the Lost Airfield Back to Life

What started as a casual interest in a Tintin location and a couple of abandoned control towers has evolved into a cross-disciplinary experiment spanning GIS, 3D modeling, urban history, and flight simulation.

The paradox of Kemayoran is that it is infinitely more compelling to rebuild precisely because it is gone. If the physical airport were still standing, there would be no mystery to solve. Because it has been swallowed by the modern city, digital reconstruction becomes the only way to recover its spatial history.

The physical towers on Benyamin Sueb Road may eventually succumb to time and vegetation, but in the digital realm, Kemayoran doesn’t have to vanish. It can fly again.
